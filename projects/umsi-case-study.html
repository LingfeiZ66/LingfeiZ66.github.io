Turning a Fragmented Ecosystem into a Connected System

What if the problem isn't a lack of resources—but the inability to find them?

Developing a product concept that earned 3rd place in a 10-day case study competition by reframing talent loss as a system navigation problem.

Overview

In a 10-day case study competition at the University of Michigan School of Information, my team developed a product concept to address a real-world challenge: why Michigan struggles to retain entrepreneurial talent. Our solution—a centralized mobile app for entrepreneurs—earned 3rd place and an honorable mention among competing teams. But the real value of this project was not just the final product. It was how we translated a complex, invisible system problem into a clear, structured solution.

The Challenge

Michigan faces ongoing talent loss, especially among entrepreneurs and young professionals. Many leave for states with stronger startup ecosystems, better resources, and more opportunities. At first glance, this looks like a resource problem. But through research, we discovered something deeper.

My Role

  • Team Lead — guided direction, facilitated decision-making, and kept the team aligned under tight timelines
  • Designed the information architecture (IA) and low-fidelity wireframes
  • Co-defined user flows and overall product structure
  • Helped translate research insights into a cohesive system-level solution

The Invisible Problem

Michigan does not lack resources. It lacks connection. Entrepreneurs face:

  • Fragmented information across different platforms
  • Low awareness of available funding, mentorship, and support
  • Disconnected networks between universities, investors, and organizations

The ecosystem exists—but it is difficult to navigate and access.

Research & Insights

We used a mix of qualitative and quantitative methods:

  • Survey (25 responses) to understand what influences people to stay or leave Michigan
  • Expert interview with Lou Glazer (President of Michigan Future, Inc.)
  • Secondary research on entrepreneurial ecosystems and policy

Key Insight Access is not the problem — navigation and connection are. Entrepreneurs are not failing because resources don't exist. They struggle because those resources are scattered, hidden, and uncoordinated.

Design Principle

Turn a fragmented ecosystem into a connected system Instead of adding more resources, we focused on:

  • Making existing resources visible
  • Connecting key stakeholders
  • Reducing friction in discovery and access

From Ecosystem → System

We reframed the problem as a system:

Key actors:

  • Entrepreneurs
  • Investors
  • Universities
  • Government agencies

Problem: These actors operate in isolated silos

Opportunity: Design a central system that connects them in one place

Solution

A Centralized Mobile App for Entrepreneurs

We designed a product that acts as a digital hub for the entire ecosystem. Instead of standalone features, each part of the app solves a system-level gap:

  • Funding Opportunities → Makes financial resources visible and accessible
  • Skill-Building Courses → Supports long-term entrepreneurial growth
  • Professional Networks → Connects users to mentors, investors, and peers
  • Local Resource Mapping → Helps users discover nearby incubators and services
  • Event Calendar → Encourages participation in the ecosystem

These features work together to reduce fragmentation and increase accessibility.

Design Process

1

Structuring Complexity into Clarity

My main contribution was transforming a complex system into a clear, usable structure:

  • Designed the information architecture to organize multiple resource types
  • Created low-fidelity wireframes to define layout and hierarchy
  • Helped shape user flows that guide users from discovery → action

This ensured the product was not just comprehensive, but also easy to understand and navigate.

2

Constraints & Decision-Making

This project was completed under extreme time pressure:

  • 10 days total
  • Only 4–5 days to refine the solution for the final round

We also faced conflicting early ideas about the direction of the project. To move forward, I introduced a simple but effective strategy:

  • Set a clear decision deadline (Day 2)
  • Use research and expert input to evaluate ideas
  • Align the team around one direction

This allowed us to:

  • Avoid endless debate
  • Move quickly into execution
  • Stay focused under pressure

Impact

  • 3rd place winner + $500 prize in the UMSI Case Study Competition
  • Recognized for a feasible, scalable solution to a real-world problem
  • Demonstrated the value of system-level thinking in product design
