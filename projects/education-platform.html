Designing with AI: Michigan Football VIP Experience

What happens when AI helps you design a product—but you can't fully trust it?

Designing a mobile app for Michigan Football VIP season ticket holders while critically evaluating how AI can support—and mislead—UX decision-making.

Defining the Problem

VIP users often interact with the app in high-pressure situations: • entering crowded stadium gates • finding parking quickly • accessing VIP areas before time-sensitive events In these moments, even small usability problems can create frustration and stress.

Through early framing and research, I identified three major themes:

  1. 1Users need instant and reliable access to their VIP credentials.
  2. 2The app must reduce uncertainty during fast-moving game-day situations.
  3. 3VIP benefits need to feel actionable and valuable, not hidden or confusing.

Research Goals & User Perspective

To better understand the experience, I explored how users currently access tickets and VIP information during game day. I focused on identifying moments where users feel slowed down, confused, or uncertain.

The research questions included:

  • How do users currently access tickets and VIP entry?
  • What creates friction during game-day situations?
  • What makes a mobile VIP ID feel trustworthy and reliable?
  • Which benefits do users actually use, and what prevents usage?

The project also explored emotional factors behind the experience. For example, users described embarrassment when IDs failed to load while people were waiting behind them in line. Others described frustration when weak signals prevented them from accessing important information quickly. These insights helped frame the app not simply as a utility tool, but as a product that must support confidence and reduce stress under pressure.

Designing with AI

One of the central goals of this project was to explore how AI could assist UX workflows while also understanding its limitations. Rather than treating AI as a replacement for design thinking, I approached it as a collaborator that could accelerate structure, brainstorming, and early synthesis.

I used AI to:

  • generate early PRD drafts
  • organize usability observations
  • explore prototype directions
  • create measurable success metrics
  • accelerate brainstorming and iteration

At the same time, I critically evaluated where AI outputs became misleading, shallow, or incomplete.

Usability Testing: AI vs Human Analysis

To better understand the strengths and weaknesses of AI-generated UX analysis, I compared AI-generated usability insights with my own human-led interpretation of the same usability testing session. This became one of the most important parts of the project.

Human-Led Analysis

During the usability test, I identified several issues that reflected deeper usability and expectation problems.

1. Scroll Expectation Problem

The participant immediately attempted to scroll on the home screen, expecting additional content below. Although the screen technically contained the necessary information, the layout visually suggested that content was incomplete. This revealed a mismatch between the interface structure and the user's mental model.

2. Parking Guidance Copy

The participant reacted negatively to the phrase: "We recommend parking closer to the stadium." She felt the statement was obvious and not genuinely helpful. Instead of offering actionable guidance, the wording repeated information users already knew. This suggested that the app needed more meaningful decision support, such as availability, distance, and timing context.

3. Missing Profile Structure

The participant could see their name within the interface but could not identify where account details or profile management existed. This created a feeling that part of the system was missing.

AI-Generated Analysis

The AI-generated analysis correctly identified several surface-level successes:

  • the VIP ID flow was easy to access
  • the brightness feature was appreciated
  • parking guidance was discoverable
  • benefits with clearer instructions stood out most

However, the AI framed the home screen experience much more positively than my own interpretation. For example, the AI concluded that: "The home screen helped the participant quickly understand the app's purpose." While technically true, this interpretation underweighted the participant's early confusion and failed to recognize the significance of the scrolling behavior.

Key Insight

The comparison revealed an important distinction: AI was effective at identifying explicit statements, but weaker at interpreting implicit expectations and deeper UX meaning. This became a critical lesson throughout the project.

From Insights to Product Decisions

The usability findings directly shaped the evolution of the product. Rather than treating testing as validation, I used it to identify where the product structure failed to match user expectations.

Problem → Decision Examples

Problem:

Users felt the home screen looked incomplete.

Design Decision:

Added a "Game Day Essentials" section to create a more complete and scannable structure.

Problem:

Parking guidance felt generic and unhelpful.

Design Decision:

Replaced generic recommendations with arrival-based logic, lot availability, and distance information.

Problem:

Users could not locate profile/account information.

Design Decision:

Added a dedicated profile screen with clear navigation access.

Problem:

Users were unsure how to use certain VIP benefits.

Design Decision:

Added "where to go," "what to show," and "when to use" instructions for each benefit.

Prototyping with AI

AI was also used to explore multiple prototype directions. Rather than generating a single design immediately, I used AI outputs to compare different assumptions about user behavior and interaction patterns.

Three main directions emerged:

1. Dashboard-Oriented Experience

Focused on freedom and visibility by showing all tools upfront.

2. Guided Assistant Experience (Selected Direction)

Focused on reducing stress by surfacing the "next best action."

3. Timeline-Based Journey

Organized the experience chronologically across game-day stages.

After evaluation, I selected the guided assistant direction because it best aligned with the product goals: • reduced cognitive load • clearer prioritization • stronger support during high-pressure moments

Final Solution

The final product focused on creating a fast, reliable, and confidence-building game-day experience.

Core Features

Fast VIP ID Access

The VIP ID is accessible within one tap from the home screen and includes automatic brightness support to improve scan reliability at stadium gates.

Clear VIP Benefit Guidance

Each benefit includes actionable instructions explaining: • where to go • what to show • when to use the benefit

Smart Parking Guidance

Parking recommendations include availability, distance, and arrival-time context to support better decisions.

Game Day Essentials Hub

A centralized section organizes important actions into a single, scannable area.

Profile & Account Access

A dedicated account section improves clarity and system completeness.

Evaluation & Iteration

The prototype was evaluated through moderated usability testing using a think-aloud protocol. Participants were asked to: • access their VIP ID • locate parking information • understand VIP benefits • navigate account-related features

The testing confirmed several strengths:

  • the VIP ID flow felt fast and intuitive
  • visual hierarchy was clear
  • users appreciated the brightness feature

However, testing also uncovered important issues:

  • expectations around scrolling
  • unclear benefit instructions
  • missing account navigation

These findings directly informed the next design iteration.

Impact

UX Impact

The final design reduced friction during high-pressure moments and improved clarity across key tasks. Users were able to:

  • access VIP IDs faster
  • understand benefits more clearly
  • navigate the system with greater confidence

Product Impact

By making VIP access feel smoother and more reliable, the design helps reinforce the perceived value of VIP membership and supports long-term renewal intent.

Reflection

This project fundamentally changed how I think about AI in UX design.

I learned that AI is extremely effective at:

  • accelerating structure
  • summarizing obvious patterns
  • speeding up early ideation

However, AI consistently struggled with:

  • nuance
  • implicit user behavior
  • prioritization
  • deeper interpretation of UX problems

The most important lesson was that AI often sounds convincing even when its understanding is incomplete.

The biggest risk isn't that AI is wrong—it's that it sounds right while being incomplete.

Designing with AI is not about replacing designers. It is about knowing when to trust AI, when to challenge it, and when human judgment matters most.
